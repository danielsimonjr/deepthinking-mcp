---

**Summary for humans:** The Halting Problem is proven undecidable by Turing's 1936 diagonalization: assume a total decider **H** exists that always correctly answers "does M halt on w?" We then construct machine **D** that takes any TM encoding ⟨M⟩, asks H whether M halts on its own encoding, and does the *opposite* — looping if H says halt, accepting if H says loop. Running D on its own encoding ⟨D⟩ forces a logical paradox in both directions: D halts ↔ D doesn't halt. The contradiction is structural and inescapable, so H cannot exist.

The argument's architecture mirrors **Cantor's diagonal argument** (1891): by inverting the diagonal of a hypothetical decision matrix, D guarantees it cannot be any row of the matrix, proving the enumeration is impossible. Importantly, HALT_TM is still **semi-decidable** — a Universal Turing Machine *accepts* whenever M does halt, it just loops forever on non-halting inputs — so undecidability means "no total algorithm," not "no information."
