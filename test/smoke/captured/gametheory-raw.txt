**Yes, there is a unique Nash equilibrium: both microservices choose short TTL, landing at payoffs (−2, −2).** Short TTL is a *strictly dominant strategy* for each service — regardless of what the other does, a service always earns more by refreshing its cache aggressively, so no unilateral deviation is ever rational.

The painful irony is that this equilibrium is **Pareto-dominated by mutual long TTL** at (+1, +1). Both services would be measurably better off if they both restrained themselves, but neither can safely deviate first — switching to long TTL unilaterally drops you from −2 to −3 while your rival benefits. This is the Prisoner's Dilemma / Tragedy of the Commons playing out over shared backend capacity.

**Practical escape routes** all involve mechanisms that externalize the cost back onto each service: a platform-enforced TTL policy via service mesh (converting the game to a cooperative one with binding rules), a shared adaptive rate-limiter on the backend (making congestion costs immediately visible), or circuit-breaker feedback loops that automatically force both services to long TTL when the backend degrades — achieving the cooperative outcome emergently without requiring trust between teams.
